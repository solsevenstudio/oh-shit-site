# What "value" actually means in service management

*A working definition, three bad ones to avoid, and a test you can run in your next meeting.*

---

Ask ten service management leaders what "value" means and you'll get eleven answers. Some will say cost. Some will say outcomes. Some will quote the ITIL 4 definition verbatim ("the perceived benefits, usefulness and importance of something") and leave it there.

None of those answers is wrong, exactly. But none of them help you make a decision on a Tuesday morning when the board wants to know whether to fund your service catalogue refresh.

So here's the working definition I've been using for years — the one I come back to every time the conversation starts to drift.

**Value is what a service does for the person it's meant to serve, measured from where they sit, not from where you sit.**

That's it. Twenty-two words. Most of the arguments I see in service management — in IT generally — come from teams measuring value from where *they* sit and then wondering why the business thinks IT is a tax.

Let me show you what that looks like in practice.

---

## Three bad definitions of "value" — and why they're seductive

**Bad definition 1: Value is cost reduction.**

It sounds disciplined. It shows up well in board papers. But "we reduced our cost per ticket by 18%" is a fact about your P&L, not about the person opening the ticket. If the tickets are still taking eight days to close, the person on the other end doesn't care that each one now costs less. Cost reduction is a *constraint*. Value is a *result*.

**Bad definition 2: Value is whatever the SLA says.**

SLAs are useful. SLAs are necessary. SLAs are not value. I've seen teams hit 99% of their SLAs while customer satisfaction fell every quarter. That's because the SLA measured "did we answer the call within X minutes" rather than "did we actually solve the problem." The SLA became the goal; the goal was forgotten.

**Bad definition 3: Value is what the customer says it is.**

Close, but not quite. The customer's *stated* preference is often different from their *revealed* preference. Ask a user what they want from IT, they'll say "faster Wi-Fi." Watch what they actually do, and the real pain is that the VPN drops six times a day and they've stopped trusting it. If you build what they said, you'll miss what they meant.

Real value lives between stated need and revealed behaviour. Your job is to triangulate.

---

## A test you can run in your next meeting

Next time someone at your work uses the word "value" — in a review, in a business case, in a planning session — ask one question:

**"Value to whom?"**

Not combatively. Just curiously. Watch what happens.

Half the time, the answer will be vague ("well, the business"), which tells you the work hasn't been grounded in a specific user or outcome yet. The other half, someone will name a real person or team — and suddenly the conversation gets useful, because now you can ask the follow-up: "and how will we know they felt it?"

"Value to whom" is the cheapest diagnostic tool in service management. Use it weekly.

---

## The test in reverse: "From where they sit"

Here's a story I tell often.

A financial services firm I worked with had a service desk team running itself into the ground. Ticket volume up. SLA performance up. Cost per ticket down. Every metric green. And yet the business complaint rate was the highest it had ever been.

What was happening? The service desk was optimising for *their* definition of value — tickets closed quickly. But the business's definition was "I got back to doing my job." And those two things had diverged. Tickets were being closed without the underlying issue being resolved, because closing the ticket hit the SLA faster than fixing the problem.

We didn't need to change the metrics. We needed to change the *frame*. "From where they sit" was the unlock. Once the team started measuring resolution from the user's side of the screen — "did you get back to work?" — the dashboard changed. So did the behaviour. So, eventually, did the complaint rate.

---

## Why this matters right now

Service management is under more scrutiny than at any point in my 30-year career. CFOs want to know why IT costs so much. CIOs want to know why their teams are exhausted. Engineers want to know why nobody understands what they do. In the middle of all of it is a discipline — service management — whose entire job is to make the invisible visible.

We can't do that job with woolly language. "Value" is the most important word in our vocabulary, and it's also the one we use most carelessly.

So let me ask you the same question I asked the team in that financial services firm:

**The last thing you shipped, launched, or delivered — what did it do for the person it was meant to serve, measured from where they sit, not from where you sit?**

Write the answer down. If you can't, you've found your next conversation.
