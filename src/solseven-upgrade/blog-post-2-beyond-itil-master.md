# Why ITIL 4 Master isn't the end of the journey

*What the certifications give you, what they don't, and where the real craft begins.*

---

I'm an ITIL 4 Master. I'm also an ITIL Ambassador. If you'd asked me twenty years ago whether those letters would be the summit of my career, I'd probably have nodded. They meant I'd arrived.

They didn't, and I hadn't. That's the thing the certifications don't tell you on the day you pass.

This post is for anyone who has either reached the top of the ITIL ladder and is quietly wondering "what now?", or hasn't started yet and wants to know what the climb is actually for. Short version: it's worth doing. And also — it's the beginning, not the destination.

---

## What the certification gives you

Let me be specific, because I've watched too many people be vague about this.

**A shared vocabulary.** This is the single most underrated thing ITIL gives you. When I'm in a room with a head of ops from a Japanese bank, a service designer from a Dutch insurer, and a CIO from an American retailer, we can talk about "service value chain" or "continual improvement" and mean roughly the same thing. That shared language saves days of work on every engagement.

**A structured framework.** ITIL is a scaffolding. It's not the building, and it's not the architecture. But when you're walking into a service management mess — legacy everything, no documentation, six different ticketing tools — ITIL gives you a set of prompts that are genuinely useful: what are the practices? where are the handoffs? who's accountable for what? It's a checklist for the things that are usually wrong.

**Credibility with stakeholders who need it.** This one matters more in some organisations than others. In a highly regulated environment, in government, in financial services — a room full of people with ITIL Master after their name is a room where the procurement team can relax. That's not nothing.

**A community.** itSMFUK, SDI, SRVision, the ITIL Ambassadors network — these are real, useful communities of practitioners. Passing the exams is the cover charge. What you get for free afterwards is a decade of peer relationships that'll shape how you think.

---

## What the certification doesn't give you

Here's the uncomfortable bit.

**It doesn't give you judgement.** ITIL can tell you that a change advisory board is a good idea. It can't tell you when to let a change go through the CAB, when to fast-track it, and when to tear the CAB up and start again because it's become a political committee. Judgement is slower to acquire than knowledge, and it only comes from doing the work — getting some of it wrong, and being honest about why.

**It doesn't give you taste.** Every service management leader I admire has a *feel* for what's going to work in a particular culture. They can walk into a team and tell within half an hour whether it's an over-processed team that needs simplicity, or an under-structured team that needs guardrails. ITIL is the same regardless. The application is where the craft lives — and the application needs taste.

**It doesn't give you courage.** This is the one I see people struggle with most. You'll be in a room where a senior stakeholder is demanding something that, if you ship it, will destroy trust with a department you need on your side for three other things. ITIL doesn't help you here. The only thing that helps you here is the willingness to say "no, and here's why." That's not on the exam.

**It doesn't give you humanity.** Service management is, at its core, about people. The user who can't log in. The engineer on their sixth incident in a row. The CIO carrying too much. The certifications teach you to think about "users" as an abstraction. The craft is in remembering they're not.

---

## Where the real climb starts

Here's the map I wish someone had drawn for me at the top of the ITIL ladder.

**1. Go sideways before you go up.**

After Master, the instinct is to look for a bigger title. Try the opposite. Take on an adjacent discipline — security, architecture, product, design, finance. The best service management leaders I know all have a second language. Mine is enterprise value co-creation. Yours might be SRE, or regulatory compliance, or organisational design. Pick one and go deep.

**2. Get paid to have your mind changed.**

The easiest way to stop learning is to become the expert in the room. The best thing you can do after Master is deliberately put yourself in rooms where you're not the expert. Join a BSI committee. Speak at a conference outside your industry. Mentor a graduate and let them ask the stupid questions that turn out not to be stupid. Each of those interactions will reshape something you thought you knew.

**3. Teach.**

Not to show off. To find out what you actually understand. The moment you try to explain value co-creation to a 22-year-old who's never heard of ITIL, you'll discover which parts of your mental model are solid and which parts are air. Teaching is the fastest way to find the air.

**4. Write.**

Even badly. Even privately. The act of writing down what you believe about service management will force you to notice the contradictions in what you believe about service management. I didn't fully understand my own views on "value to whom" until I'd written the same blog post four times and thrown three of them away.

**5. Accept that the discipline is still being invented.**

Service management in 2026 is not what it was in 2016, and it won't be what it is in 2036. Generative AI is rewriting how we do knowledge management. Hybrid work has broken the desk-side support model. The old "three levels of support" doctrine is wobbling. ITIL Master is a certification in the discipline *as it was*. The real craft is in helping invent what comes next.

---

## A closing thought

The day I passed ITIL Master, my brilliant old boss sent me a one-line message: "Great. Now forget it."

He wasn't being dismissive. He meant: you've absorbed the framework — now stop thinking about it. Stop quoting chapter and verse. Use it in the background, as a set of instincts, and put your attention back on the work and the people.

Twenty years on, it's the best career advice I've ever received.

If you're mid-climb, keep going — the certification is genuinely worth having. And when you get to the top, look up. There's a whole career above it.
